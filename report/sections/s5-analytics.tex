\section{Analytics Methods}
\subsection{Outbreak detection (AN-1, AN-3)}
Both detectors run per district and disease on a series prepared in Spark (\texttt{analytics/detection/}).
First, every week a district reported nothing at all is marked missing, as is any week where fewer than 80\% of
its expected facilities reported; above that, the count is scaled by $1/\text{completeness}$. Second, counts
are divided by a seasonal index per division, disease and week of year, taken as the median over the
\emph{other} years, so an outbreak cannot raise its own expected value.

EARS C2~\cite{hutwagner2003ears} compares each week with the mean and standard deviation of lags 3 to 9 and runs
on Spark window functions. CUSUM~\cite{page1954cusum} accumulates standardised excesses over a 26-week baseline behind a two-week
guard band and runs in \texttt{applyInPandas}. Neither may alarm unless the last four weeks hold at least five
cases, which keeps near-empty series quiet. Farrington~\cite{farrington1996} was ruled out: it needs about five
years of history before its first test week, and we have five in total.

The reporting artefact is handled twice: gold counts by onset week, so back-filled records land on the weeks
they happened, and the silent weeks are missing rather than zero.

\subsection{Forecasting (AN-4)}
For dengue and diarrhoea, one model per district and horizon $h=1,\dots,4$:
\begin{multline}
\log\mu_t = \beta_0 + \textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{2} \text{Fourier}_k(t) + \beta_r\,\text{rain}_{t-\ell} \\
+ \beta_T\,\text{temp}_{t-\ell} + \beta_y \log(1+\text{level}_{t-h})
\end{multline}
with lags $\ell$ of four to eight weeks and $\text{Var}(y)=\mu+\alpha\mu^2$ (NB2~\cite{hilbe2011nb}), fitted with
statsmodels. The baseline is the seasonal-naive forecast, the value of the same week a year earlier~\cite{hyndman2021fpp3}.
Because every weather lag is at least as long as the horizon, no weather forecast is needed. Districts are
fitted in parallel through a cogrouped \texttt{applyInPandas}. Predictive quantiles are the plug-in NB
quantiles, which ignore parameter uncertainty.

\subsection{Demand, allocation and calendar (AN-6, AN-7, AN-9)}
Forecast cases become demand for beds, IV saline, NS1 kits, ORS and antimalarials through admission shares
estimated from the data, lengths of stay and units per case or admission, all in
\texttt{demand\_assumptions.py} and marked as our assumptions. The LP plans against the 90th percentile.

The allocation is the brief's LP solved with PuLP~3.3.2 and CBC, with two changes of ours. First, unmet need
for each resource is divided by its national net need, so a bed and a test kit are not worth the same in one
objective. Second, each district's unmet need is priced in four equal slices with weights 1, 2, 3 and 4, so the
last quarter of a shortfall costs most. With a single flat price every unit of a resource is worth the same in
every district; the LP is then indifferent between districts and returns a corner, with some districts fully
supplied and the rest given nothing (Section~\ref{sec:alloc}). Net need is the four-week 90th-percentile demand minus current
stock; for beds the patients already admitted are counted as holding a bed, so they are not counted twice. With
$\alpha=0$ the LP is pure efficiency; with $\alpha=1$ every resource is split by population. The model is
feasible for every $\alpha$ because $x=0$ satisfies every constraint.

The preparedness calendar fits a seasonal-trend decomposition by loess (STL)~\cite{cleveland1990stl} to each division and disease, inside \texttt{applyInPandas}. Onset is the first week
after the trough where the seasonal index passes 25\% of the trough-to-peak range; supplies are pre-positioned
three weeks before onset and campaigns start four to six weeks before it.

\subsection{Epidemic scenarios (AN-10)}
A deterministic SEIR model~\cite{kermack1927} (\texttt{scenario/seir.py}) is fitted to the national
respiratory surge. The surge is found without the ground truth: weekly ILI counts minus the median of the same
week in the other years, keeping the run of weeks around the largest excess. With latent and infectious
periods fixed at three and five days, least squares fits $R_0$, the seed and the share of infections that are
reported. Scenarios then cut contacts by 20\% or 40\% from the first surge week, or two weeks later.
