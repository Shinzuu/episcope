\section{Evaluation}\label{sec:eval}
Every number in this section comes from one clean SF=1 build (seed 42, real weather; 34.1 million encounters,
of which 1.37 million, 4.0\%, were quarantined) from a fresh clone of the final code, with the result files
in \texttt{docs/experiments}. The exceptions are the scaling runs (Table~\ref{tab:scaling}) and the
before-and-after measurements of the fixes, which say where they come from.

\subsection{Detection}
\texttt{make evaluate} scores every alert against the 35 injected outbreaks in \texttt{ground\_truth/}, the only
code allowed to read that folder. Consecutive alert weeks merge into one episode. An episode that overlaps no
outbreak window counts as a false alert. The sweep picks, for each method, the most sensitive setting that keeps
false alerts at or below two per district-year (conflict C1, Fig.~\ref{fig:tradeoff}). Table~\ref{tab:detection}
shows the chosen operating points.

EARS C2 is the default: it finds one outbreak more and finds them sooner, at a similar false-alert rate. CUSUM's
alerts are more often right (PPV 0.25 against 0.15). By shape, EARS C2 found 31 of 33 fast outbreaks, the slow
one after 41 days and the nationwide surge after one day; CUSUM found 30 fast ones, the slow one after 69 days
and the surge after one day. We expected CUSUM to win on slow growth. On our one slow outbreak it did not.
Both methods missed the same outbreak, \texttt{OB42-017}. At SF=0.25 the same EARS threshold gave fewer false
alerts (1.27 per district-year), because smaller counts make random spikes rarer. The threshold should be
re-tuned on real counts.

The reporting artefact (a facility that goes silent and then back-fills) is checked by a test: a naive detector
on report-date counts alerts on the back-fill week, while ours, on onset-week counts with silent weeks missing,
does not.

\subsection{Forecast backtest}
The backtest refits the per-district NB-GLM at 52 rolling origins (every fourth origin, reused in between) and
forecasts one to four weeks ahead using only data up to each origin. The seasonal-naive baseline is scored on
the same district, disease, origin and horizon cells. The weighted interval score~\cite{bracher2021wis}
uses eleven central intervals plus the median; relative WIS below 1 means the model beats the baseline. MAE is
the mean absolute error of the median forecast.

The model beats the baseline at every horizon for both diseases, by more for diarrhoea (relative WIS 0.72)
than for dengue (0.89). Its 80\% intervals cover 79\% of outcomes, against 77\% for the baseline, so the bands
mean what they say. For dengue, 105 of the model's WIS of 130 is underprediction: the model is slow to follow a
season's rise. That matters for stock, and it is why the plan uses the 90th percentile, not the mean.

\subsection{Allocation against the population baseline}\label{sec:alloc}
The first allocation runs were degenerate: generated stock was 3 to 225 times the four-week demand, so almost
nothing needed allocating, and the LP sent what little there was to two districts.
\texttt{allocation\_diagnosis.md} traces three faults: stock that ignored the case load, an objective that
valued every district equally (so the solver returned a corner: in a test with need everywhere, 21 districts got
all their beds and 42 got none), and beds counted twice. After the fixes, at SF=0.25 and half of the money
needed, all 64 districts receive supplies, the five largest receive 22\% of the value instead of 100\%, and the
Gini of per-capita spend falls from 0.98 to 0.27.

At SF=1 the national net need is 43,558 litres of IV saline, 513,721 ORS sachets and 691 antimalarial courses;
NS1 kits and beds need no top-up, because the data end in December, after the dengue season. Covering all of it
costs 7.78 million BDT ($B_{\text{ref}}$). Table~\ref{tab:alloc} compares the LP with the population-proportional
baseline at five budgets.

The LP meets about twice the share of need at every scarce budget. The efficiency and equity curve
(\texttt{allocation\_curve.csv}) gives the price of equity: at a quarter of $B_{\text{ref}}$ a floor of up to

floor costs more than two points, so the rule recommends $\alpha=0$. The tranche prices already spread the
money: at $\alpha=0$ and half the budget the worst-covered district gets half of its need.

\subsection{Epidemic scenarios}
The SEIR fit to the nationwide respiratory surge (nine weeks from 28 October 2024) gives $R_0=2.04$ with
$R^2=0.91$; the generator used 1.8. Cutting contacts by 20\% from the first surge week lowers the peak by 46\%
and the cases over the surge and the following 12 weeks by 18\%; a 40\% cut lowers them by 87\% and 63\%.
Starting the 40\% cut two weeks late still lowers the peak by 80\% but the cases by only 53\%, which is the
case for acting on the first alert (\texttt{seir\_scenarios.json}).

\subsection{Scalability and Spark optimisations}
\texttt{scripts/scaling.sh} builds each scale factor from a fresh clone and times every stage
(Table~\ref{tab:scaling}). The laptop has an Intel Core i5-8350U (4 cores, 8 threads) and 15.4\,GB of RAM;
Spark runs as \texttt{local[4]} with a 5\,GB driver. The scaling runs (20 and 21 October) predate the
allocation fix and the second optimisation below; the last column is the final SF=1 build, also from a fresh
clone (\texttt{sf1\_final\_run.json}). SF=1 finishes in 25 to 28 minutes, inside the 45-minute gate. Silver
grows with the row count and is the slowest stage. Analytics hardly grows, because it works on weekly series
per district, not on rows. A first SF=1 attempt failed in silver when Spark's spill filled the small
RAM-backed \texttt{/tmp}; spill now goes to a disk folder.

Two optimisations were measured, each with its outputs checked unchanged.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Silver evaluates the quality rules once.} The 14 encounter rules, including the duplicate-ID rule
that sorts the whole load, were evaluated once for every output (quarantine, its count, the quality report and
the clean MERGE). They are now evaluated once and persisted. At SF=0.25 silver's Spark time fell from 325\,s to
214\,s, and every silver table kept the same row count and row hash (\texttt{silver\_optimisation.json}).
\item \textbf{The backtest uses every core.} The Spark UI showed the backtest's model stage running as a single
task for 5.1 minutes (Fig.~\ref{fig:sparkui}): its input is a few megabytes, so adaptive query execution had
merged the shuffle into one partition and fitted all 64 districts one after another. Keeping one partition per
district for that job gives 64 tasks. At SF=0.25 the job fell from 325\,s to 165\,s with identical scores;
at SF=1 the backtest step fell from 365\,s to 161\,s (\texttt{backtest\_optimisation.json}).
\end{itemize}
