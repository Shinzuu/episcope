\section{Policy Brief for the Director}
\textbf{To:} Director, Disease Control \& Preparedness Unit, Ministry of Health. \textbf{About:} what the EpiScope prototype
can do for weekly outbreak warning and supply planning, and what to decide.

\subsection*{Where we are}
EpiScope is a working prototype that watches all 64 districts every week for four diseases: dengue,
diarrhoea, influenza-like illness and malaria. It warns when a district's cases rise more than expected,
forecasts the next four weeks with a likely range, turns those forecasts into the supplies hospitals will need,
and proposes how to share a fixed budget between districts. It also gives each division a calendar of when to
prepare for each disease season. It was tested on realistic made-up patient records (34 million visits over
five years) built on Bangladesh's real population and weather, because no real patient data was used. One
ordinary laptop processes the whole country in about 25 minutes.

\subsection*{Three recommendations}
\textbf{1. Act on the weekly warning list.} In testing, the list caught 33 of 35 planted outbreaks, half of
them within eight days. The price is false alarms: about two a year in each district. Each district should
expect to check a couple of warnings a year that turn out to be nothing, and should check every one, because a
missed outbreak costs far more. For a fast-spreading respiratory virus, acting on the first warning matters
most: in our model, cutting contacts by 40\% from the first warning prevents about 63\% of cases, against 53\%
if the same step comes two weeks later.

\textbf{2. Send supplies where the need is, not by population.} When there is only half of the money needed,
sharing supplies by the measured shortfall in each district covers 68\% of the shortfall, averaged over the
supplies; sharing the same money by population covers 34\%. The dashboard shows each supply separately, so a
single supply can sit above or below that average. The dashboard lets you set a minimum share for every district (the equity
floor): each district then gets at least that fraction of its population share of what is sent, but never more
than it needs, so no supplies go where there is no shortfall. At a quarter or three quarters of the money a
minimum share changes the shortfall covered by less than one point; at half the money it costs 1.5 to 2.4 points. This is a policy choice, and the dashboard shows its cost before you make it.

\textbf{3. Prepare before each season, not during it.} The calendar gives each division a week to have supplies
in place and a week to start prevention work (Table~\ref{tab:brief-calendar}). For dengue in Dhaka division, supplies should be in place by
mid-July and the prevention campaign should start in late June, three and six weeks before the season usually
begins in early August. Malaria matters mainly in Chattogram division (the three Hill Tracts districts and Cox's Bazar): supplies
should be in place there by 4 May, with the campaign from 13 April.

\subsection*{Decision requested}
Approve the supply plan for the next four weeks. Meeting every district's expected shortfall costs about
7.8 million taka. With the full amount, every shortfall is covered. With half of it (3.9 million taka), the
need-based plan covers 68\% of the shortfall (averaged over the supplies) and a population-based split 34\%. Decide also whether
every district gets a guaranteed minimum share (its population share, never more than it needs): at half the
money the recommended setting is no minimum, because no minimum also gives the worst-off district the most; a
minimum would lower the share of need covered by 1.5 to 2.4 percentage points.

\subsection*{How to read the dashboard}
The line at the top lists the districts on alert in the last full week. Choose a disease below it. The map
shades each district by cases per 100,000 people; a solid triangle marks a warning in the last full week, an
outlined one a warning earlier. Click a district to see its weekly cases, its forecast for the next four weeks as a table of
expected cases and a likely range (eight times in ten the real number falls inside it), and its place in the
warning list. The newest week is drawn as an open dot because its reports are not all in yet. The allocation panel shows how
the chosen budget is shared; move the sliders to see what a larger budget or a higher minimum share would do.
District officers open a view of their own district, with the rest of the country shown only as division
totals.

\subsection*{How far to trust it}
The test data were made up, so these results show that the methods work on realistic patterns, not that they
will match real outbreaks to the day. The forecasts tend to fall short when dengue is rising fast, so stock is
planned on the high end of the range, not the middle. Supply costs and the amount used per patient are our
estimates and should be replaced with ministry figures. A warning is a reason to investigate, not proof of an
outbreak.

\subsection*{How patient data are protected}
Names and ID numbers never leave the secure store: each patient is replaced by a code that cannot be turned
back into the number, and ages become age groups. Only totals reach the dashboard, and any count from one to
four is hidden, together with any other number that could reveal it. A district officer sees their own district
in detail and the rest only as division totals.

\section{Individual Reflections}\label{app:reflections}
\subsection*{Nishadul Islam Shezan}
I led the data engineering: the synthetic data generator, ingestion into the Bronze layer, the Silver and Gold
Delta tables, incremental loads, and the data-quality and lineage checks. The problem I remember most came at full
scale: the temporary folder held in memory ran out of space and the Silver step blew past the time limit, so I moved
the temporary data to disk and profiled and rewrote the slow step until the full run fitted. Every load can now be
re-run without double-counting.

\paragraph*{Reflection} My role was data engineering: the synthetic data generator, ingestion into the Bronze layer, the Silver and Gold
Delta tables, incremental loads, and the data-quality and lineage checks. Early on I learned that a pipeline that
works on a laptop sample tells you very little. The real lessons came at full scale, where a temporary folder in
memory ran out of space and a slow Silver step had to be profiled and rewritten before the run fitted the time
limit. I now design for re-runs from the start: every load can be repeated without double-counting, and every Gold
row can be traced back to the batch it came from.

In the final weeks my teammates were away, so I also implemented the remaining parts of their areas, working from
the designs we had agreed in the proposal. That taught me how much a clear data contract saves: because the
tables and their columns were fixed early, I could build detection, forecasting and the dashboard on top without
redesigning anything. If I started again, I would set up the scaling test in the first week instead of the last,
because performance problems shaped more of the design than I expected.

\subsection*{Tahsin Ahmed Rafi}
\paragraph*{My part} I led the analytics: EARS C2 and CUSUM outbreak detection on seasonally adjusted counts,
the per-district negative-binomial forecasts with lagged weather, the conversion of forecasts into supply demand,
the allocation optimiser, and the dengue preparedness calendar.

\paragraph*{Problem and how I solved it} Two facilities that went silent for three weeks and then back-filled
everything would have triggered false alarms. I marked a week as missing when fewer than 80\% of expected
facilities reported and scaled the count up otherwise. Together with onset-week counting in gold, the back-fill
spike never reaches the detector.

\paragraph*{Reflection} I led the analytics side of EpiScope: outbreak detection, forecasting, and the optimisation that turns forecasts
into a supply plan and a preparedness calendar. The part I found most interesting was choosing between methods
that look similar on paper. EARS C2 is built for a sudden jump and CUSUM for a slow rise, but on our one slow
outbreak CUSUM was the slower of the two (69 days against 41), which taught me to test a textbook claim instead
of assuming it.

Forecasting taught me to be careful with evaluation. A model can look accurate until it is tested only on data
that comes after its training period, which is why the backtest matters more than the training fit. For the
allocation step, I learned that the objective function is really a policy choice: deciding how much weight to give
fairness against total need met is a question for the Ministry, not only for the solver.

I had to step back in the final phase, and Shezan completed the implementation of my area from our design. If I did
this again, I would write the evaluation plan before building any model, so that the measures are fixed before the
results are known.

\subsection*{Rimjhim Dey}
\paragraph*{My part} I led the visualisation side of EpiScope, which is everything between the analysed
data and what a health officer sees. That covers three things:
\begin{itemize}
\item The serving layer (\texttt{serving/export.py}). It turns the gold tables and analytics results into small
aggregated JSON files of at most 10 MB for the dashboard, with every count from 1 to 4 suppressed.
\item The D3.js dashboard. It has five linked views: a district map, an alert table, time series, a seasonal
heatmap and a supply-allocation view. It also has a what-if panel with an alert-level slider and a budget control.
\item The district views (\texttt{serving/roles.py}). A district user sees their own district in detail and every
other area only as a division total.
\end{itemize}
These cover requirements VIS-1 to VIS-7, PRV-2 (suppression in outputs) and PRV-3 (role-based views).

\paragraph*{Problem and how I solved it} The main problem was privacy. Hiding every count below five is not enough,
because a hidden number can be worked out by subtracting the visible cells from a row, column or division total.
I solved it in two steps.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Complementary suppression. If a row or column had exactly one hidden cell, I also hid its smallest visible
cell, so no single hidden cell can be recovered by subtraction. Derived values such as incidence rates are hidden
wherever their count is hidden, because rate times population would give the count back.
\item District views. A division total shown next to a district's own hidden cell would leak it. So a district user
sees the total of their division's other districts only, and an aggregate is published only if every district cell
under it is visible. A district user can't reconstruct a hidden number by comparing or subtracting the figures they
are shown.
\end{enumerate}
The alert-level slider had the same problem. Exporting the alarm gate would have exposed hidden counts, so the
dashboard recomputes it in the browser from the published counts only.

\paragraph*{Reflection} My area was visualisation: the dashboard, the serving layer behind it, and the district views with small-number
suppression. What stayed with me most is that a dashboard for public-health officers is not about showing
everything. A district officer needs to see, at a glance, which districts need attention this week and why, and
anything beyond that gets in the way. We kept the map, the alert list and the supply view simple for that reason.

Privacy changed how I think about charts. Hiding a count below five is easy, but a hidden number can often be
worked out from the totals around it, so the other cells have to be hidden as well. I had not realised how many
ordinary-looking tables can give away a single patient until we worked through those cases.

I was away for the final part of the project, and Shezan finished the dashboard and the suppression code from our
plan. Looking back, I would test the dashboard with real users much earlier, because people who are not from
computer science read colours, labels and maps very differently from the people who built them.

\subsection*{Samira Sultana Mysha}
\paragraph*{My part} I worked on the platform and evaluation part of EpiScope. I focused on evaluating the
system's outbreak detection, forecasting accuracy, privacy protection, and performance at different data scales.

\paragraph*{Problem and how I solved it} One challenge was balancing early outbreak detection with avoiding too
many false alarms. I addressed this by testing different alert thresholds against planted outbreaks and comparing
the results using detection rate, false alarms, and detection time to choose suitable thresholds.

\paragraph*{Reflection} I was responsible for the platform and evaluation side: problem analysis and the proposal, de-identification and
the k-anonymity check, the evaluation and backtest plan, orchestration and testing, and the report and policy
brief. Working on the proposal showed me how many conflicts sit inside a single requirement. Detecting outbreaks
early, protecting patient privacy and keeping false alarms low all pull in different directions, and the design
only became clear once we wrote those trade-offs down.

The privacy work was the most valuable for me. Replacing a patient identifier with a keyed hash looks simple, but it
only protects people if the key is kept out of the code and the data, and if the released tables are also checked
for small groups that could identify someone. Evaluation taught me a similar lesson: a result is only as good as the
test that produced it.

During the final phase I could not continue, and Shezan completed the remaining platform and evaluation work. If we
did the project again, I would agree on the test cases and the evaluation measures at the very start, so that
everyone builds towards the same definition of done.
