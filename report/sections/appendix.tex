\section{Policy Brief for the Director}
\textbf{To:} Director, Disease Surveillance and Resource Planning. \textbf{About:} what the EpiScope prototype
can do for weekly outbreak warning and supply planning, and what to decide.

\subsection*{Where we are}
EpiScope is a working prototype that watches all 64 districts every week for four diseases: dengue,
diarrhoea, influenza-like illness and malaria. It warns when a district's cases rise more than expected,
forecasts the next four weeks with a likely range, turns those forecasts into the supplies hospitals will need,
and proposes how to share a fixed budget between districts. It also gives each division a calendar of when to
prepare for each disease season. It was tested on realistic made-up patient records (34 million visits over
five years) built on Bangladesh's real population and weather, because no real patient data was used. One
ordinary laptop processes the whole country in about 25 minutes.

\subsection*{Three recommendations}
\textbf{1. Act on the weekly warning list.} In testing, the list caught 33 of 35 planted outbreaks, half of
them within eight days. The price is false alarms: about two a year in each district. Each district should
expect to check a couple of warnings a year that turn out to be nothing, and should check every one, because a
missed outbreak costs far more. For a fast-spreading respiratory virus, acting on the first warning matters
most: in our model, cutting contacts by 40\% from the first warning prevents about 63\% of cases, against 53\%
if the same step comes two weeks later.

\textbf{2. Send supplies where the need is, not by population.} When there is only half of the money needed,
sharing supplies by the measured shortfall in each district covers 68\% of the shortfall; sharing the same
money by population covers 34\%. The dashboard lets you set a minimum share for every district (the equity
floor). When money is very short, a modest floor costs little; at half the money it costs more than two points
of the shortfall covered. This is a policy choice, and the dashboard shows its cost before you make it.

\textbf{3. Prepare before each season, not during it.} The calendar gives each division a week to have supplies
in place and a week to start prevention work. For dengue in Dhaka division, supplies should be in place by
mid-July and the prevention campaign should start in late June, three and six weeks before the season usually
begins in early August.

\subsection*{How to read the dashboard}
Choose a disease at the top. The map shades each district by cases per 100,000 people, and a triangle marks a
warning. Click a district to see its weekly cases, its forecast for the next four weeks with a shaded band (the
real number will usually fall inside it), and its position in the warning list. The allocation panel shows how
the chosen budget is shared; move the sliders to see what a larger budget or a higher minimum share would do.
District officers open a view of their own district, with the rest of the country shown only as division
totals.

\subsection*{How far to trust it}
The test data were made up, so these results show that the methods work on realistic patterns, not that they
will match real outbreaks to the day. The forecasts tend to fall short when dengue is rising fast, so stock is
planned on the high end of the range, not the middle. Supply costs and the amount used per patient are our
estimates and should be replaced with ministry figures. A warning is a reason to investigate, not proof of an
outbreak.

\subsection*{How patient data are protected}
Names and ID numbers never leave the secure store: each patient is replaced by a code that cannot be turned
back into the number, and ages become age groups. Only totals reach the dashboard, and any count from one to
four is hidden, together with any other number that could reveal it. A district officer sees their own district
in detail and the rest only as division totals.
